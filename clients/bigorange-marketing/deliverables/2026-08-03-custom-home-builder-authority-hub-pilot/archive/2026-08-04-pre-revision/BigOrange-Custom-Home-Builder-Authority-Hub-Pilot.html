Section 05 · Technical SEO and AI Search Specification

Technical SEO and AI Search Implementation Specification

Prepared August 3, 2026 for the existing BigOrange WordPress page at https://bigorange.marketing/marketing-agency-for-builders/.

Decision

Keep and evolve the existing URL. Do not publish a second page targeting the same central topic. Preserve the current page's useful builder proof and internal-link equity while replacing its architecture, metadata, copy hierarchy, and structured-data graph.

Current public baseline

  • Live status: HTTP 200.
  • Canonical: self-referencing and correct.
  • Visible body copy: approximately 2,057 words in the August 3 inventory.
  • Headings: 43 H1–H3 elements; one H1.
  • Internal-link instances: 83.
  • Images: 34; two lacked alt text in the rendered inventory.
  • Structured data: one JSON-LD block detected.
  • Robots policy: OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, and PerplexityBot are allowed; GPTBot, ClaudeBot, and Google-Extended are disallowed. That is a defensible search-access versus model-training distinction and should be reviewed intentionally rather than changed reflexively.

Lighthouse lab baseline

Single lab runs captured August 3, 2026. These are diagnostic snapshots, not CrUX field data.

The immediate risk is mobile loading and main-thread work. Official good Core Web Vitals thresholds are LCP at or below 2.5 seconds, INP at or below 200 milliseconds, and CLS at or below 0.1 at the 75th percentile. Field data should determine the actual pass/fail status; Lighthouse TBT is only a lab proxy for responsiveness.

WordPress implementation requirements

URL and publishing

  • Edit the existing page in staging or as an unpublished revision.
  • Preserve the current slug and self-canonical.
  • Do not change the URL unless a redirect map, internal-link update, canonical QA, and Search Console monitoring plan are approved.
  • Record pre-launch HTML, screenshots, metadata, schema, and Lighthouse evidence for rollback.

Metadata

  • Proposed title: Custom Home Builder Marketing Agency | BigOrange Marketing
  • Proposed meta description: A practical marketing system for custom home builders: positioning, website conversion, SEO, local and AI visibility, content, proof, and measurement.
  • H1: Custom Home Builder Marketing That Builds Trust Before the First Call
  • Social title and description should match the page promise without unsupported result claims.
  • Use a builder-specific Open Graph image with descriptive alt text.

Content and navigation

  • Add an anchored table of contents after the introduction.
  • Use one H1 and a logical H2/H3 hierarchy.
  • Put a short direct answer under every question-style heading before the detail.
  • Add “last reviewed,” author, and SME reviewer fields.
  • Link to the three subpillars and the first two supporting articles with descriptive anchor text.
  • Preserve useful existing case examples, testimonials, awards, and package context only when their wording remains approved and attributable.
  • Add a final source note distinguishing official guidance, BigOrange experience, and client-specific evidence.

Media and accessibility

  • Resize hero and case-study images to their rendered dimensions.
  • Prefer AVIF or WebP with JPEG fallback where the existing stack supports it.
  • Never lazy-load the LCP image; preload only the single real LCP candidate.
  • Lazy-load below-the-fold media.
  • Add meaningful alt text for informative images and empty alt text for decorative images.
  • Preserve visible focus, semantic landmarks, keyboard access, sufficient contrast, and reduced-motion behavior.
  • Re-run automated and manual accessibility checks before publishing.

Performance work order

  1. Identify the mobile LCP element and its request chain.
  2. Remove any hero video, carousel, or background image from the critical path; use a lightweight poster or static image first.
  3. Eliminate render-blocking CSS and unused Elementor/widget CSS on this page.
  4. Delay nonessential third-party scripts until consent or interaction where appropriate.
  5. Reduce JavaScript execution and long tasks; audit sliders, animation libraries, chat, tracking duplication, and tag-manager containers.
  6. Serve critical fonts locally where licensing permits, subset weights, and use font-display: swap.
  7. Confirm WP Rocket and Cloudflare caching behavior on anonymous mobile requests.
  8. Test on a clean mobile connection after cache warm-up and cold-cache conditions.
  9. Validate with PageSpeed Insights/CrUX and Search Console Core Web Vitals after launch.

Internal linking

  • Pillar links down to every subpillar and primary supporting article.
  • Every supporting asset links back to the pillar in the first meaningful third of the copy.
  • Cross-link adjacent assets only where the link answers the reader's next question.
  • Add breadcrumbs and keep link anchor text descriptive.
  • Remove accidental duplicate links caused by desktop/mobile widget copies if both are present in the DOM.

Structured-data graph

Use a single coherent @graph, not disconnected duplicate objects from multiple plugins.

  • Organization: stable BigOrange identity and approved profiles.
  • WebSite: publisher relationship and site identity.
  • WebPage: canonical URL, title, description, dates, breadcrumb, and primary image.
  • Article: author, reviewer, dates, headline, image, publisher, and mainEntityOfPage where the final page qualifies as editorial content.
  • BreadcrumbList: Home → Industries or Services → Custom Home Builder Marketing.
  • FAQPage: only for questions and answers that are visible on the page. Do not expect or promise a Google rich result.
  • Optional VideoObject: only when a public on-page video has a stable thumbnail, duration, upload date, and transcript.

Schema must match visible content. Do not add review, rating, service-area, award, price, or result claims that are absent from or unsupported on the page.

AI-search implementation

There is no special “AI ranking” tag. Google states that its normal SEO foundations apply to AI features; OpenAI and Perplexity provide crawler controls for search discovery. The controllable program is:

  1. Keep the page indexable and snippet-eligible.
  2. Allow intended search crawlers through robots.txt and the CDN/WAF.
  3. Use clear entity names, audience, services, markets, and authorship.
  4. Answer specific builder questions in visible text.
  5. Support important claims with original evidence or reputable citations.
  6. Keep structured data consistent with the visible page.
  7. Build internal links and external corroboration around the same entity and topic.
  8. Track qualified referral traffic and assisted outcomes instead of promising placement.

Measurement plan

Search and discovery

  • Search Console clicks, impressions, CTR, average position, indexed pages, and query mix for the cluster.
  • Bing Webmaster Tools equivalent visibility.
  • Organic landing sessions and engaged sessions in GA4.
  • Referrals from ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, Copilot, and other discoverable AI sources where referrer data is available.
  • A monthly, versioned prompt set for directional mention/citation observation. Treat outputs as volatile samples, not rankings.

Business outcomes

  • Qualified consultation submissions.
  • Sales-accepted leads.
  • Service-area, project-type, budget, and timing fit.
  • Assisted conversions and return visits across the long decision cycle.
  • Pipeline and closed-won value only when attribution and CRM definitions are validated.

Conversion reporting is pending validation until GA4, Search Console, HubSpot, form, call, booking, and CRM outcome definitions are reviewed together.

Pre-publish QA

  • Approved content and Janice SME revisions applied.
  • One H1; no skipped heading levels that impair comprehension.
  • Metadata, canonical, robots, sitemap, and status code verified.
  • Schema passes syntax checks and matches visible content.
  • All internal and external links tested.
  • Form, thank-you, call tracking, and consent behavior tested.
  • 390px, 768px, 1024px, and 1440px visual QA complete.
  • Keyboard and screen-reader spot check complete.
  • Fresh mobile and desktop performance captures archived.
  • Rollback snapshot and responsible publisher recorded.

Section 08 · AI-Assisted Editorial Workflow

AI-Assisted Editorial Workflow

Operating principle

AI accelerates organization and variation. It does not supply BigOrange's experience, client proof, market facts, or final judgment. Every published asset must have a named human owner, traceable sources, and an approval record.

One-hour SME interview to multi-asset system

Inputs

  • Approved content brief and search intent.
  • Existing BigOrange pages, case evidence, and brand-voice rules.
  • One recorded and transcribed 60-minute interview with Janice.
  • Approved official and industry sources.
  • Professional keyword export when available.

Production sequence

  1. Frame. Define audience, business question, primary query, reader outcome, CTA, prohibited claims, and source requirements.
  2. Interview. Ask for decisions, mistakes, examples, objections, and the phrases builders actually use.
  3. Extract. Separate the transcript into facts, opinions, examples, quotable lines, open questions, and confidential material.
  4. Verify. Check every number, named example, result, timeline, regulation, and platform claim against its original source.
  5. Architect. Build the answer-first outline, internal links, evidence slots, FAQ, and CTA before drafting prose.
  6. Draft. Use AI for a sourced first draft, alternatives, summaries, metadata, and repurposing. Require it to mark missing evidence instead of filling gaps.
  7. Human edit. Janice or the assigned SME checks technical truth; the editor checks voice, usefulness, originality, search intent, and reader flow.
  8. QA. Run fact, plagiarism, link, accessibility, schema, metadata, visual, and mobile checks.
  9. Publish. Use a named human publisher, version note, approved date, reviewer, and rollback path.
  10. Learn. After 30, 60, and 90 days, compare actual query mix, engagement, qualified actions, and sales feedback; update the asset rather than producing near-duplicates.

Prompt contract

Every production prompt should include:

  • audience and funnel stage;
  • one primary job the content must do;
  • approved source packet with dates and URLs;
  • BigOrange voice rules;
  • required heading and internal-link structure;
  • claims that must not be made;
  • explicit instruction to write [SOURCE NEEDED] or [SME VALIDATION] when evidence is absent;
  • output format and length range;
  • a claim table at the end of the working draft.

Example drafting instruction

Draft for a busy custom home builder evaluating marketing support. Use only the attached approved sources and transcript. Start each section with a direct answer. Do not invent a quote, metric, customer result, search volume, ranking, or platform capability. Mark unsupported statements [SOURCE NEEDED]. Preserve BigOrange's warm, practical, lightly playful voice. Return the article plus a claim-and-source table for human review.

Why custom home builder marketing is different

A custom home is not an impulse purchase. The decision is emotional, financial, local, and highly personal. Buyers are not only comparing floor plans or galleries. They are asking whether you will listen, communicate, protect the budget, navigate tradeoffs, and still be there when the easy part is over.

That creates a longer trust journey:

  • A referral or search result introduces the name.
  • The website helps the buyer decide whether you fit their project and values.
  • Reviews, project stories, and third-party proof reduce perceived risk.
  • Helpful answers show how you think before the buyer shares private details.
  • A clear consultation process makes the next step feel safe.
  • Consistent follow-up carries trust through a decision that may take months.

Marketing works when those moments reinforce one another. It struggles when each channel tells a different story—or when a beautiful website leaves the buyer with the same unanswered questions they had before visiting.

Start with the buyer and your real difference

The fastest way to sound like every other builder is to start with words such as quality, integrity, craftsmanship, and dreams. Those ideas matter. They also mean very little until you show what they look like in your process.

A useful position answers five questions:

  1. Who is the right client? Define project type, geography, investment range, design involvement, and working style without trying to appeal to everyone.
  2. What moment brings them to you? They may be moving, rebuilding, aging in place, escaping a poor renovation experience, or finally acting on a long-held plan.
  3. What do they fear? Common concerns include budget drift, silence, decision overload, quality uncertainty, and a process they cannot see.
  4. How does your process reduce that risk? Name the checkpoints, communication rhythm, decision tools, and people involved.
  5. What proof supports the promise? Use specific projects, client language, awards, credentials, partnerships, timelines, and documented practices—with permission and context.

This is not a branding exercise that ends with a clever line. It becomes the filter for the website, photography, content, ads, sales conversations, and the leads you choose to pursue.

What is custom home builder marketing?

Custom home builder marketing is the coordinated work of positioning a builder, earning visibility, explaining the buying and building process, proving credibility, and turning right-fit interest into qualified conversations. It usually connects the website, search, local presence, content, reviews, project proof, paid distribution where appropriate, and sales follow-up.

How is custom home builder marketing different from general contractor marketing?

The buyer, economics, proof, project timeline, and decision risk are different. A custom-home strategy must support a longer, more personal consideration process and explain design collaboration, investment fit, communication, and trust—not only service availability.

Should a builder rely on referrals?

Referrals are valuable and often high quality, but they do not provide predictable control over timing, market reach, or project fit. Search, content, proof, and follow-up should strengthen referrals and create additional discovery paths.

How long does SEO take for a custom home builder?

There is no reliable universal timeline. Technical condition, current authority, competition, geography, content quality, implementation speed, and sales-cycle length all matter. What a Custom Home Builder Website Must Include

A custom home builder website has one serious job: help the right prospective client trust your process enough to take the next step.

Pretty matters. But a gallery alone cannot answer the questions running through a buyer's head: Do they build in my area? Is my project a fit? Who will guide me? How do decisions work? Will they communicate? What happens if the budget and the wish list disagree?

The strongest builder websites answer those questions clearly, prove the answers, and make contact feel like a sensible conversation rather than a funnel ambush.

1. A first screen that names the fit

Within a few seconds, a visitor should understand:

  • what kind of builder you are;
  • who you serve;
  • where you work;
  • the kind of project or relationship you are best suited for;
  • the next reasonable step.

“We build dreams” does not do enough work. A clearer opening might identify custom homes, a real service area, design-build or architect collaboration, and the consultation path.

Keep the first screen focused. Large sliders, autoplay video, multiple competing buttons, and vague award claims can hide the message and slow the page—especially on a phone.

2. Projects that tell a decision story

Photography proves taste and capability. Context proves judgment.

For selected projects, explain what the homeowner wanted, what made the site or design challenging, which choices mattered, and how the team moved the work forward. Use approved details. If a budget, location, or homeowner identity is confidential, protect it.

A useful project story can include:

  • project type and general market;
  • the homeowner's goal;
  • one meaningful constraint;
  • a design or construction decision;
  • the builder's role;
  • approved photography and captions;
  • a review or result with source and timeframe.

This turns a portfolio from “look what we made” into “see how we think.”

3. A process buyers can picture themselves inside

Custom building feels risky when the process is invisible. Explain the major stages in plain language:

  1. fit and discovery;
  2. site, design, or feasibility review;
  3. scope and investment alignment;
  4. selections and planning;
  5. construction communication;
  6. handoff and follow-through.

Do not invent certainty where the work is variable. The purpose is to show how decisions and communication are handled, not to make every project sound identical.

4. Real people and a real point of view

Buyers are choosing a working relationship, not only a set of services. Introduce the people who will guide the project. Use current photographs, useful roles, and short biographies that explain how each person helps the client.

A clear point of view also helps. If the team believes early budget conversations protect the design, say so and explain why. If communication has a defined rhythm, show it. If certain project types are not a fit, clarity is kinder than a vague promise.

5. Qualification information that saves everyone time

Qualification does not have to feel cold. It can help a buyer understand whether a conversation is likely to be productive.

Consider explaining:

  • service geography;
  • project types;
  • typical planning horizon;
  • design or architecture relationships;
  • land or site expectations;
  • investment guidance where the team is comfortable and the information can stay current;
  • what the first conversation covers.

If exact price ranges would mislead, explain the factors that shape investment and the point at which the team can responsibly discuss it.

6. Trust signals with enough context

Reviews, awards, credentials, partnerships, and press can reduce risk. They work best when the visitor can understand what they mean.

  • Link to the source where appropriate.
  • Name the awarding or reviewing organization.
  • Add dates when relevance changes over time.
  • Use exact customer language only with permission.
  • Avoid blending unrelated clients or implying a result from one project applies to everyone.

Trust is not a strip of logos at the bottom of a page. It is the consistency between the promise, the proof, and the process.

7. Answers for the research phase

Most prospects do not arrive ready to book. Give them useful answers before asking for personal information.

Strong topics include:

  • how to choose a custom home builder;

  • how the design and budget process interact;
  • how long planning may take;
  • what makes a project a fit;
  • how builders, architects, and designers collaborate;
  • what local conditions shape a project.

Answer directly, then add nuance. Avoid stretching a simple answer into a long article just to create word count.

8. A helpful next step

“Contact us” makes the visitor do too much guessing. A stronger CTA explains:

  • who the conversation is for;
  • what happens after the form;
  • who responds;
  • what information is useful;
  • whether there is any obligation;
  • the expected response window, if the team can support it.

Offer a practical alternative for visitors who are not ready, such as a planning checklist or a short guide.

A credible story still fails if the page is painful to use.

Test the live site on a real mobile viewport and connection. Compress images, keep the main visual out of an unnecessary slider or video dependency, reduce script work, avoid layout shifts, and make the text readable without pinching or zooming.

Use one clear H1, descriptive headings, meaningful link text, keyboard access, visible focus, sufficient color contrast, accurate alt text, and form labels. Structured data should identify the page, organization, author, breadcrumb, and visible FAQ where appropriate—but it must match what people can see.

5 Articles Every Custom Home Builder Blog Needs

The best builder blog is not a pile of seasonal posts or a scrapbook of completed homes. It is a patient sales teammate.

It answers the questions buyers are nervous to ask, shows how the team thinks, and gives a qualified prospect a reason to come back before they are ready for a consultation.

If you are starting or cleaning up a custom home builder blog, these five articles create a useful foundation.

1. How our custom home process works

This is not a generic six-step graphic. Give the reader a practical view of how your process handles uncertainty.

Explain:

  • what happens before a formal engagement;
  • when land, architecture, design, and construction expertise enter the process;
  • how investment and scope are discussed;
  • when major decisions happen;
  • how communication works;
  • what changes from project to project.

The reader does not need every internal detail. They need enough visibility to decide whether your approach feels thoughtful and compatible.

Questions the article should answer

  • What should we prepare before the first conversation?
  • Do we need land or plans already?
  • Who owns design decisions?
  • How do we know whether our goals and investment align?
  • What happens after we decide to move forward?

Use a real process owner to review the article. If your sales page and your actual process disagree, fix the gap before publishing.

2. What affects the investment in a custom home?

Avoiding every cost conversation does not make the concern disappear. It sends the buyer elsewhere for answers—and those answers may be misleading.

A useful investment article can explain the variables without pretending to price a unique project online:

  • site conditions and preparation;
  • design complexity;
  • size, geometry, and structural choices;
  • finish and system selections;
  • permitting and local conditions;
  • schedule and market volatility;
  • landscaping, external work, and allowances;
  • the difference between an early estimate and a construction-ready price.

If the team publishes ranges, add the geography, project assumptions, date, inclusions, and review cadence. If ranges would be unreliable, explain when a responsible number becomes possible and what information is required.
