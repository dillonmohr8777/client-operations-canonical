# Commercial Cleaning Checklist for Facility Managers

**A useful commercial cleaning checklist identifies every space, the work required there, how often it happens, who owns it and how completion will be checked.** That structure turns a vague request to “keep the building clean” into a service plan that a facility manager and cleaning partner can consistently inspect.

## Start with the building, not a generic task list

Walk the facility and group areas by how they are used. A practical inventory usually includes entrances, offices, shared workspaces, restrooms, breakrooms, meeting rooms, corridors, storage areas and exterior approaches. Note the flooring, traffic level, hours of use, restricted-access areas and surfaces that need manufacturer-specific care.

## What should the checklist include?

For each area, document:

1. **Task:** the observable action, such as emptying waste, damp-wiping a counter or vacuuming carpet.
2. **Frequency:** per shift, daily, weekly, monthly, seasonally or as needed.
3. **Method and product:** including dilution, label directions, required personal protective equipment and surface compatibility.
4. **Owner:** cleaning team, day porter, facility staff or a specialty vendor.
5. **Quality standard:** the condition an inspector should see, not merely a checked box.
6. **Exceptions:** secure rooms, occupied spaces, events, weather or maintenance work that changes access.

The [CDC’s facility guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/when-and-how-to-clean-and-disinfect-a-facility.html) recommends cleaning visibly dirty surfaces before disinfecting and following the product label, including the required contact time. That distinction belongs in the method column whenever disinfection is part of the approved scope.

## Match frequency to risk and traffic

Entrances and restrooms may need attention more than once per day, while a low-use conference room may need routine service only after use. Shared touchpoints, food areas and visibly soiled surfaces deserve a defined response. Periodic work—floor refinishing, high dusting, interior glass and detailed edge cleaning—should appear on a calendar so it does not disappear behind daily tasks.

Floor inspection also supports safety. [OSHA’s walking-working-surface rule](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.22) requires workplaces to be kept clean, orderly and, to the extent feasible, dry. Your plan should therefore include spill response, warning signs and prompt reporting of damaged surfaces.

## Add an inspection loop

A checklist becomes useful when it produces feedback. Assign a weekly or monthly walkthrough, record recurring misses and update the scope when occupancy or operations change. Use specific notes—“soap dispenser empty” or “soil remains at west entry edge”—instead of subjective labels.

## Frequently asked questions

### How detailed should a commercial cleaning checklist be?

Detailed enough that two trained people can interpret the expected result the same way. Name the space, task, frequency, method and quality check without turning the document into an unreadable procedure manual.

### Should disinfecting appear on every checklist?

Only where the facility’s policy, risk assessment or approved scope calls for it. Routine cleaning and disinfection are different actions; product labels and facility requirements control the method.

### How often should the checklist be updated?

Review it whenever occupancy, use, flooring, equipment or facility policy changes, and at least during regular service reviews.
