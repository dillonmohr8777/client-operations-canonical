## Week 1: Back-to-school stability

Week of August 3, 2026

### 2026-08-04 · Truth Tuesday

**A Schedule Is Not the Same as a Routine**

A parenting schedule can explain where children will be. A routine helps everyone understand how the week will actually work.

School-night expectations, transportation, homework, activities, medication, and the items that move between homes can all affect whether a plan feels calm or chaotic. The more predictable those details become, the less children have to carry in their heads.

A written schedule is important. A usable routine is what helps families live it. As the school year begins, focus on the small details that create consistency across two homes.

**Five Back-to-School Details to Put in One Place**

Back-to-school planning gets easier when the important details are not scattered across texts, emails, backpacks, and memory.

Create one shared place for the school calendar, transportation plans, activity schedules, emergency contacts, health information, and the supplies children need in each home. Then decide how updates will be communicated when something changes.

The goal is not perfect coordination. It is fewer last-minute surprises and less pressure on children to act as messengers. One reliable source of information can make the entire week easier to follow.

Children should not have to manage the entire transition between two homes on their own.

## Week 2: Mediation preparation and steady progress

Week of August 10, 2026

### 2026-08-11 · Truth Tuesday

**Mediation Is a Process, Not a Promise**

Mediation creates a structured setting for people to discuss decisions with the help of a neutral mediator. It does not guarantee that every issue will be resolved, and the mediator does not take either person's side.

The value of the process is the opportunity to organize the conversation, identify the decisions that need attention, exchange information, and explore options outside of an adversarial courtroom setting. The people involved remain responsible for their decisions.

Mediation may be one path forward, but whether it fits depends on the people, issues, and circumstances involved.

**Prepare for Mediation by Organizing the Conversation**

Preparation for mediation is not about scripting the perfect argument. It is about making the conversation easier to understand.

Begin by listing the decisions that need attention. Gather the information connected to those decisions, note the questions you need answered, and identify which concerns matter most to you. It can also help to separate your underlying priorities from the specific position you first had in mind.

Organized information gives everyone a clearer starting point. Your attorney or mediator can explain what preparation is appropriate for your circumstances and the process you are using.

## Week 3: Choosing a process and celebrating the team

Week of August 17, 2026

### 2026-08-18 · Truth Tuesday

**Family-Law Processes Are Not One Size Fits All**

There is not one single path for every divorce or separation.

Families may resolve decisions through direct settlement conversations, collaborative divorce, mediation, litigation, or a combination of approaches as circumstances change. Each process has a different structure, and the right fit depends on the issues, safety considerations, communication dynamics, and goals involved.

Choosing a process should be an informed decision, not a reaction to the loudest advice online. A family-law attorney can explain the available options and help you understand which questions matter in your circumstances.

### 2026-08-19 · Wednesday Wisdom

**Three Questions to Ask Before Choosing a Process**

Before choosing how to approach a family-law matter, start with the decisions and circumstances rather than the label attached to a process.

Ask what decisions need to be made, how much structure the conversations may require, and whether any concern could affect safety, access to information, or the ability to participate fairly. Also consider whether both people are willing and able to exchange information and work toward resolution.

These questions do not select the process by themselves. They create a more useful conversation with a qualified family-law professional about the available options.

**A Parenting Plan Should Be Specific Enough for a Busy Week**

A parenting plan can look complete while still leaving families with unanswered questions.

Real life includes delayed pickups, school events, activities, appointments, weather, forgotten items, and schedule changes. A useful plan addresses the recurring details clearly enough that families do not have to renegotiate the same issue every week.

Specific language is not about controlling every moment. It can reduce avoidable confusion and give parents a dependable starting point when the week becomes busy. A qualified professional can help identify which details belong in a plan for your circumstances.

## Week 6: Clear communication and supportive adults

### 2026-09-08 · Truth Tuesday

**A Good Co-Parenting Message Has One Clear Purpose**

A long message does not always create a clearer conversation.

When possible, keep a co-parenting message focused on one topic. Include the information needed to understand the issue, identify the decision or response requested, and avoid adding older disagreements that do not need to be solved in the same exchange.

Focused messages can be easier to answer and less likely to create new confusion. If communication feels unsafe, threatening, or persistently unworkable, speak with a qualified professional about the options available in your circumstances.

**Four Parts of a Clear Schedule-Change Message**

Schedule changes are easier to evaluate when the request contains the information that matters.

Explain what changed, state the specific adjustment you are requesting, identify when an answer is needed, and describe any practical effect on transportation, school, activities, or the child. Keep the tone neutral and the message limited to the current decision.

A clear request does not require the other person to agree, and it does not override an existing order or agreement. It simply creates a better starting point for the conversation.

## Week 7: Financial organization and manageable decisions

Week of September 14, 2026

### 2026-09-15 · Truth Tuesday

**Financial Organization Is Not the Same as Financial Agreement**

Gathering financial information is an important part of many family-law conversations, but organization is not the same as agreement.

Bank statements, income information, account records, debts, expenses, and property documents can help people understand what exists and which questions remain. That information still needs to be evaluated in the context of the specific matter, applicable law, and each person's goals.

Do not confuse a well-organized spreadsheet with a final legal decision. Use organized information to ask better questions and seek qualified advice before making an agreement.

**Five Record Categories to Gather Before a Financial Conversation**

A financial conversation is easier to understand when the underlying records are organized.

Depending on the matter, useful categories may include income and tax records, bank and investment accounts, loans and other debts, property and insurance documents, and recurring household or child-related expenses. Keep the records complete, readable, and grouped by category.

The documents needed in a particular legal process can vary. Ask your attorney, mediator, or other qualified professional what is appropriate before sending sensitive financial information and use a secure delivery method.
